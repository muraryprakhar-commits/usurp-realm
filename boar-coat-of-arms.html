What a boar means on a coat of arms

Courage, above all the kind that does not run. A wild boar is not a hunter like the lion or the wolf, but when it is cornered it turns and fights, and medieval hunters knew it as one of the most dangerous animals in the forest. That is why the boar came to stand for a warrior who holds his ground. Crest Studio's own meaning for it is "a fierce fighter who never retreats".

The boar was a symbol of strength long before heraldry began. The Benty Grange helmet, a 7th-century Anglo-Saxon helmet found in Derbyshire in 1848, has a small boar standing on its crown, and the poem Beowulf describes boar images on warriors' helmets as a kind of protection. In Old English the word for boar was eofor, and it survives in names: Everett comes from Everard, an old personal name made of words for "boar" and "brave" or "hardy", the same name as the German Eberhard.

So a boar on a shield says something blunter than an eagle or a raven. It is about grit, loyalty to your own and refusing to give way. That makes it a good emblem for a family that has come through hard times, a rugby or hockey team, a hunting or outdoors club, or a barbecue joint with a sense of humor.

The boar's head: heraldry's favorite way to draw a boar

Whole boars do appear on shields, standing (statant) or striding (passant), but heraldry far more often shows just the head. Head charges are among the oldest in heraldry, and the boars' heads in the punning arms of the Swinburne family (swine) are recorded from at least 1255.

A head is either couped, cut off with a clean straight line, or erased, torn off with a jagged edge. There is also a well-known national difference. In English heraldry a boar's head traditionally comes with its neck, cut at the shoulders. In Scottish and Welsh heraldry it is cut off close, just behind the ears, with no neck at all. The tusks are its "arms", so a blazon may say a head is armed argent, with silver tusks, and langued gules, with a red tongue. Crest Studio draws a boar's head couped in the English style, side-on with its bristled neck, and blazons it that way. The guide to reading a blazon explains the rest of the vocabulary.

Famous boars in history and heraldry

The white boar of Richard III

The best-known boar in English history is the white boar, the personal badge of King Richard III, who reigned from 1483 until he was killed at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485. Badges like this were worn by a lord's followers, and a record survives of an order for 13,000 white boar badges in fustian cloth for Richard's coronation and his son's investiture as Prince of Wales. One theory is that the boar was a pun on "Ebor", short for Eboracum, the Latin name for York. In 2009 a small silver-gilt boar badge was found near the marsh where Richard is thought to have died, and it helped archaeologists pin down where the battle was really fought. His motto was Loyaulte me lie, "loyalty binds me".

The Campbells and the Gordons

Two of the great Highland clans carry boars. The crest of the Campbell chief, the Duke of Argyll, is a gold boar's head, and clan members wear it in their crest badge with the motto Ne Obliviscaris, "forget not". Clan tradition links the boar to Diarmid, a hero of Gaelic legend who was killed by a boar. The Gordon chiefs bear three gold boars' heads on blue, with the motto "Bydand", an old Scots word meaning steadfast or abiding. These arms and crests belong to the chiefs, so treat them as inspiration rather than something to copy. The Scottish family crest guide explains how clan crest badges work.

Bacon and the boar

In 1568 English heralds granted Sir Nicholas Bacon, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal and father of the philosopher Francis Bacon, a crest of a boar passant in ermine. It looks like the perfect pun on the name, though some historians trace it to his Quaplode ancestors instead. The family motto is Mediocria firma, usually translated "moderate things are stable" or "the middle course is safest".

The boar's head feast

A boar's head carried in on a platter was once the grand dish of an English Christmas. The "Boar's Head Carol", a 15th-century carol in mixed English and Latin, was printed in 1521, and The Queen's College, Oxford, still serves a boar's head at its Christmas dinner while the carol is sung. It is a good reason a boar crest makes a fun winter or holiday gift for a family that loves a big table.

Designing your boar crest

Colors

Gold on blue is the classic Gordon-style pairing, bright and confident. Black on silver is stark and strong, and reads well engraved or printed in one color. Silver on red is fierce, which suits a sports team, while gold on green feels like forest and hunting lodge. Each pair keeps the rule of tincture, a metal (gold or silver) against a color. The heraldry meanings guide explains what each color traditionally stands for.

Keep it bold

A boar's head is a chunky shape with a lot going on: snout, tusks, ear and bristles. It reads best on a plain field. If you want a division, make sure the head sits on a metal, or keep the division colors close so the tusks do not get lost.

Canting arms for boar names

Heraldry loves a pun. If your surname is Everett, Everard, Eberhardt, Swinton, Swinford, Hogg or Bacon, a boar is the obvious choice. The German family crest guide has more on canting arms, including the boar (Eber) of the town of Eberbach.

Mottos

Plain English works well: "Stand and Hold", "Never Retreat" or "Bold and Steadfast". For Latin, try Audentes fortuna iuvat (fortune favors the bold) or Fortis et fidelis (strong and faithful). Questions

What does a boar mean on a coat of arms?

Courage and ferocity, especially the courage to stand and fight rather than run. A cornered wild boar was famous for turning on its hunters, so it became a symbol of a warrior who never retreats.

What does a boar's head couped mean?

It means the head is cut off with a clean straight line. Erased means it is torn off with a jagged edge. In English heraldry the boar's head usually includes the neck, while in Scottish and Welsh heraldry it is cut close behind the ears.

Which clans have a boar on their crest?

The Campbell chief's crest is a gold boar's head, worn by clan members in the crest badge with the motto Ne Obliviscaris. The Gordon chiefs bear three gold boars' heads on blue with the motto Bydand. Both belong to the chiefs, so use your own design.

Why was the white boar Richard III's symbol?

Nobody knows for certain. It was his personal badge, given to his supporters in large numbers. One theory is that it was a pun on Ebor, short for Eboracum, the Latin name for York.

Can I put a boar on my family crest?

Yes. The boar is a traditional heraldic charge anyone can use in an original design. Avoid copying a real family or clan coat of arms exactly, and treat a Crest Studio design as decorative art rather than official arms.
