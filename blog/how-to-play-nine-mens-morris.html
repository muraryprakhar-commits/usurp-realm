How to Play Nine Men's Morris: Rules and a First Strategy

Two players, eighteen pieces and a board of three squares. You can learn the rules in five minutes. Here they are, with pictures, plus the habits that win your first games.

The board and the pieces

The board is three squares, one inside the other, joined by four lines through the middles of their sides. Pieces sit on the 24 points where lines meet or turn, never inside the squares. Each player has nine pieces, which is where the name comes from: “morris” goes back to a Latin word for a counter or gaming piece.

The game has three phases. You place your pieces, then you move them, and a player who is down to three pieces may fly.

Phase 1: placing

Players take turns putting one piece from their hand on any empty point. That is all a turn is, until all eighteen pieces are on the board. Even now, the aim is the same as for the whole game: make mills.

Mills and taking pieces

A mill is three of your pieces in a row along one line. Each time you complete a mill, you take one of your opponent's pieces off the board, and it does not come back.

  • You must take a piece that is not part of a mill. Only when every one of your opponent's pieces is in a mill may you take one from a mill.
  • One mill takes one piece.
  • A mill counts each time you form it. Breaking a mill and closing it again later takes another piece.

Phase 2: moving

When both hands are empty, a turn means sliding one of your pieces along a line to the next point, which must be empty. There is no jumping and no moving across the middle of a square. Pieces that cannot move are blocked, and a player with no legal move at all loses the game.

Phase 3: flying

A player who is down to three pieces may fly: move any one of them to any empty point, anywhere on the board. It makes the weaker side dangerous, because a flying piece can close a mill from across the board.

How the game ends

  • You win when your opponent has only two pieces left, since two pieces can never make a mill.
  • You also win when your opponent cannot make a legal move.
  • Rules for draws vary from player to player. On this site a game is drawn when the same position comes round three times, or when 30 moves each pass without a capture.

A first strategy: five habits

1. Do not chase mills while placing

A mill made early takes one piece, but it often leaves your other pieces scattered. Spend the placing phase taking good points and blocking your opponent's rows of two.

2. Take the busy points

Corners touch only two other points. The middle point of a side touches three, and on the middle square it touches four. Pieces on busy points stay free to move once the sliding starts.

3. Count your moves before the placing ends

Being blocked loses the game, and it happens most often just after the last piece is placed. Before you place your last few pieces, check that each of them will have somewhere to slide.

4. Build two threats at once

If you have two rows of two, each waiting for a third piece, your opponent can block only one. The other becomes a mill.

5. Make a mill that opens and closes

The strongest shape in the moving phase is a mill where one piece can slide out and back in. Each time it slides back, the mill closes again and you take another piece. If your opponent builds one, block the empty point next to it straight away.
