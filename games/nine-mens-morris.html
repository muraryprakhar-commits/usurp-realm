How to play

  1. Each player has nine pieces. Take turns placing them on the empty points where the lines meet.
  2. Three of your pieces in a row along a line is a mill. Every mill you make lets you take one of your opponent's pieces.
  3. When all eighteen are placed, take turns sliding one piece along a line to the next empty point.
  4. A player down to three pieces may fly to any empty point. Leave your opponent with two pieces, or with no move, and you win.
Phone and tabletTap a point to place a piece. To move, tap your piece and then a glowing point, or drag it.
ComputerClick, or use the arrow keys to move the blue ring and Space to choose.

Tips

  • Make two-in-a-rows that the computer has to answer, so it spends its turns blocking.
  • A piece that can slide out of a mill and back in again makes a new mill every other turn.
  • Corners touch only two points; the middle of a side touches three or four. Pieces on corners are easier to hem in.
  • Keep your pieces free to move. Being blocked after the placing phase loses the game.

The game of mills

Nine Men's Morris goes by many names: mills, merels, and in German Mühle. Boards were scratched into stone benches and church seats, and cut into the turf of village greens.

It was already played in the Viking age: the game board from the Gokstad ship burial in Norway has Nine Men's Morris on one side and a tafl game on the other. The rules here are the common modern ones, including flying when a player is down to three pieces.

Questions

Can you take a piece that is in a mill?

Only when every one of your opponent's pieces is in a mill. Otherwise you must take a piece that is not.

What happens with three pieces left?

That player may fly: move any piece to any empty point, not only along a line. It makes a comeback possible.

Can the game be a draw?

Yes. Here it is a draw if the same position comes round three times, or if 30 moves each pass without a capture.

How old is Nine Men's Morris?

Very old. A Viking-age board from the Gokstad ship burial in Norway has it on one side, and by the 1300s it was one of the most played games in Europe. In Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream, Titania complains that the nine men's morris marked out on the village green is filled up with mud.
