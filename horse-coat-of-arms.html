The Horse Coat of Arms: Meaning and Design

The horse carried knights, plowed fields and pulled the mail, so it is no surprise it turns up all over heraldry. What a horse means on a coat of arms

Readiness, above all. A horse saddled and waiting is a picture of someone prepared to serve the moment they are called, which is why heraldry links it with duty, speed and loyalty. Crest Studio's own meaning for it is "readiness to serve, speed and loyalty". For most of history the horse was also the clearest sign of rank: a knight was, almost by definition, a man who fought on horseback, and the French chevalier and Spanish caballero both come from words for horse.

The horse was a powerful image long before heraldry. The Uffington White Horse, a 110 meter figure cut into the chalk of an Oxfordshire hillside, was made some time between 1380 and 550 BC, at the end of the Bronze Age or the start of the Iron Age, according to optically stimulated luminescence dating. The National Trust looks after it today, and it is far older than any coat of arms.

That gives a horse crest a warm, practical feel. It suits a family that loves riding or comes from farming stock, a stable, riding school, pony club or polo team, a family that has moved a long way to make a new life, or anyone whose motto is about being ready when it counts.

How heralds describe a horse

A horse in heraldry is described by its pose, and the words are worth knowing if you want to read old arms or write a blazon of your own:

  • Statant: standing still with all four hooves on the ground.
  • Passant: walking, with one foreleg raised.
  • Courant or in full career: galloping.
  • Salient: leaping, with both hind legs on the ground.
  • Forcené: rearing up on the hind legs. This is the heraldic word for the pose most people call rampant when they talk about a horse.

A horse may also be bridled, saddled or caparisoned, which means draped in a decorated cloth like a knight's war horse. Just as with lions and boars, heralds often show only the head. A horse's head couped is cut off with a clean straight line, and erased means torn off with a jagged edge. Older armorials sometimes call it a nag's head, which simply meant a horse. Famous horses in heraldry

The Saxon steed of Lower Saxony and Westphalia

The best-known heraldic horse in Europe is the white horse on red called the Saxon steed (Sachsenross in German). It appears in the arms of the German state of Lower Saxony and in the arms of North Rhine-Westphalia, where it stands for Westphalia, and earlier it was used by the Kingdom of Hanover and the Duchy of Brunswick. In heraldry it first shows up in the late 14th century. A tradition first written down in 1492 claimed that Widukind, the Saxon leader who fought Charlemagne in the 8th century, rode under a horse banner, but historians treat that story as a later legend rather than a record.

Invicta: the white horse of Kent

The English county of Kent has its own white horse, rearing on a red field. Its correct blazon is Gules, a stallion forcené argent, and it goes with the county motto Invicta, Latin for "unconquered". Legend links it to Hengist and Horsa, the brothers said to have led the first Anglo-Saxon settlers into Kent in the 5th century; in Old English their names mean "stallion" and "horse". The earliest known picture of that story is an engraving in Richard Verstegan's book of 1605, which shows them landing under a white horse banner. Some historians think the Kent horse and the Saxon steed grew from the same late medieval legend.

Stuttgart: a city named after a stud farm

Stuttgart's name comes from the Old High German Stuotgarten, a stud farm, because the city grew from a stud where Duke Liudolf of Swabia bred war horses around the year 950. Its arms are a black horse on gold, a perfect example of canting arms, a picture that puns on the name. The horse faced the other way in a version from 1591, and the city's modern drawing dates from 1938. The German family crest guide has more German speaking arms like this one.

Horses that pun on a name

English heralds loved the same joke. James Parker's Glossary of Terms Used in Heraldry, a Victorian reference that is still widely used, lists a Bishop Horsley who bore three silver horses' heads on red, a Colte family of Essex with three black colts running on silver, and a Scottish Trotter family whose arms show a horse trotting. They are the kind of pun you can borrow for your own crest without copying anyone's arms.

Designing your horse crest

Colors

Silver on red is the Saxon and Kent look: bold, old and easy to read from a distance. Gold on green feels like paddocks and open country, which suits a stable or farm. Gold on blue is calm and dignified for a family crest, and black on silver is crisp enough to engrave, embroider on a saddle pad or print in one color. Each pairing keeps the rule of tincture, a metal (gold or silver) against a color. The heraldry meanings guide explains what each color traditionally stands for.

Keep the head clear

A horse's head has a long, elegant outline and a mane that adds detail. It reads best on a plain field, so the shape stays clean on a stable sign, a rosette or a phone screen.

Canting crests for horse names

If your surname is Horsley, Colt, Trotter, Rider or Ross in its German sense (Ross is an old German word for a steed), a horse is the obvious choice.

Mottos

Short and plain works well: "Ever Ready", "Steadfast and True" or "Heart Over Hurdles" for a jumping yard. For Latin, try Semper paratus (always ready) or Fortis et fidelis (strong and faithful). The Latin motto generator has more, each with a translation.

Questions

What does a horse mean on a coat of arms?

Readiness to serve, speed and loyalty. Because knights fought on horseback, the horse was also a sign of rank and of being prepared to answer a call to duty.

What is the white horse on the Lower Saxony flag?

It is the Saxon steed (Sachsenross), a white horse on red. It appears in the arms of Lower Saxony and of North Rhine-Westphalia, where it stands for Westphalia, and it first shows up in heraldry in the late 14th century.

What is the horse of Kent called?

The white horse of Kent, often just called Invicta after the county motto, Latin for "unconquered". Its blazon is Gules, a stallion forcené argent: a silver horse rearing on red.

What is the difference between a horse rampant and forcen&eacute;?

Forcené is the proper heraldic word for a horse rearing on its hind legs. Rampant is the word for a lion in that pose, and people often use it loosely for horses too.

Can I put a horse on my family crest?

Yes. The horse is a traditional heraldic charge anyone can use in an original design. Avoid copying a real family, city or state coat of arms exactly, and treat a Crest Studio design as decorative art rather than official arms.
