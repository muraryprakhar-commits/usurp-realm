Start with your own crest, not someone else's

Many people search for "the family crest" of their surname and get it tattooed. The trouble is that a coat of arms historically belonged to one person or one family line, not to everyone who shares a last name. The shield sold as "the Murphy crest" or "the Smith crest" may have belonged to a family you are not related to at all, and a tattoo is a long time to wear someone else's arms.

If your family has a documented coat of arms, wonderful: research it properly before you commit. If not, the more personal choice is to design your own. Pick colors and an emblem that describe your family as it is, put your name on it, and choose a motto you actually say. The heraldry meanings guide explains what each color and emblem traditionally stands for, and the Latin motto generator has dozens of established mottos with translations.

Design rules that help a crest read on skin

A crest on a wall can carry fine detail. A crest on skin is viewed from farther away, wraps around curves and changes with the years. Experienced artists commonly warn that ink spreads slightly under the skin over time, so tiny details placed close together can blur into each other. That makes heraldry a natural fit, because good heraldry was always meant to be recognized at a distance.

  • One bold emblem. A single charge, such as an anchor, a rose, a tree, a lion or a star, reads far better than a shield crowded with symbols. The studio has 50 emblems to choose from.
  • Few words. A short family name and a motto of two to four words leave room for letters to stay legible. Small text is usually the first part of a tattoo to lose clarity, so ask your artist how large the lettering needs to be.
  • Strong contrast. Heraldry's rule of tincture, a metal (gold or silver) against a color, never color on color, keeps the design clear. In black and grey work, that becomes light against dark.
  • Simple divisions. A plain field or one clean line, such as a chevron or a split down the middle, is easier to keep crisp than complex patterns.
  • Let size follow detail. If you want more detail, plan for a larger piece. Your artist is the best judge of the smallest size a design will hold up at.

Color, black and grey, or line art

Full color

The classic heraldic look: gold, silver, red, blue, green, purple, black and mulberry. Every example on this page is built from those tinctures.

Black and grey with heraldic hatching

Heralds solved the problem of showing color in black ink centuries ago. The hatching system published by the Jesuit herald Silvester Petra Sancta in Rome in 1638, now used across Europe, gives each tincture its own pattern: dots for gold, plain for silver, vertical lines for red, horizontal lines for blue, diagonal lines for green and purple, and a grid for black. A hatched crest tells anyone who knows heraldry exactly which colors your arms have, without a drop of colored ink. It is a lovely idea for black and grey work, though fine hatching needs enough size to stay readable, so talk it through with your artist.

Line art

Outlines only, with no fills. This is the version most artists find easiest to turn into a stencil and adapt to their own style, whether that is traditional, fine line or blackwork.

Questions

Can I get my surname coat of arms tattooed?

You can, but check first. A historical coat of arms usually belonged to one family line, not to everyone with the same surname, so the "surname crest" you find online may not be connected to your family. Designing your own crest gives you something that is truly yours.

What file should I give my tattoo artist?

A vector SVG is ideal because it scales to any size without losing sharpness. Line art (outlines only) is the easiest version to turn into a stencil. The $19 Heirloom set includes line-art, two-tone and hatched engraving files as well as the full-color SVG.

Can a family crest tattoo be done in black and grey?

Yes. Heralds have shown colors in black ink with hatching since the 1600s: dots for gold, vertical lines for red, horizontal lines for blue and so on. Your artist can use the hatched version, shading, or simple line art.

How big should a family crest tattoo be?

That depends on how much detail and text you want and where it goes, so ask your artist. As a rule, more detail and smaller lettering need a bigger piece to stay clear over the years.

Am I allowed to use the design for a tattoo?

Yes. Once you buy the files you may use them for any lawful purpose, personal or commercial, including a tattoo. The design is original artwork in the style of heraldry, not an official grant of arms.
