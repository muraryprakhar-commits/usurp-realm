Why heraldry is a gift to worldbuilders

In medieval Europe, coats of arms appeared in the twelfth century, when they were painted on shields and banners and pressed into wax seals. One of the oldest surviving pictures of a coat of arms, the enamel memorial of Geoffrey Plantagenet made around 1151, shows him carrying a blue shield with small gold lions. Within a few generations arms were passed from parent to child, and a shield became a shorthand for a whole family.

That is exactly what a writer needs. A banner on a hill, a badge on a messenger's sleeve or a seal on a letter can tell your reader who is involved without a paragraph of explanation. And because real heraldry followed rules, a world whose arms follow rules of their own feels older and more lived-in than one where every house simply picks a cool animal.

Start with the world, not the shield

Before you draw anything, decide a few things about how arms work in your setting:

  • Who bears arms? In real history it was never only knights. Towns, guilds, bishops, abbeys, universities and many ordinary townspeople used arms too. In your world, maybe only sworn houses may, and carrying arms you have no right to is a crime. Either choice creates story.
  • Who controls them? England has had a College of Arms since 1484, and Scotland has the Court of the Lord Lyon. An office of heralds who register arms, settle disputes and read shields across a battlefield can be a whole faction in your plot.
  • Are they inherited? If arms pass down a family, younger children and cousins need a way to show who is who. Real heraldry solved this, as the next section shows.
  • What do they cost? Some colors were expensive to produce as dyes. A house that can afford a deep purple banner is making a statement, and a house that cannot is making a different one.

Borrow the rules that make arms feel real

Keep the rule of tincture

Heralds put a metal (gold or silver) on a color (red, blue, green, purple or black), or a color on a metal, so a shield could be read from a distance. Arms that break the rule were rare and drew comment. If you break it in your world, make it mean something: a usurper's arms, a foreign house, or a deliberate insult to tradition.

Simple is old, busy is new

The earliest arms were bold and spare: one beast, one band, two colors. Later centuries added more and more pieces as families married, merged and claimed honors. You can use that as a signal. An old house has a plain, confident shield. A newly ennobled merchant may have a crowded one.

Let the arms pun on the name

Heralds loved visual puns, called canting arms. The Spanish kingdom of Castile bore a castle, and the kingdom of León bore a lion. A house called Ashcombe might carry an ash tree, House Ravensworth a raven (the real Corbet family did exactly this, as the raven coat of arms guide explains), the Brackwaters a ship. Readers enjoy noticing these, and they help them remember who is who.

Mark the younger branches

English heralds developed small marks of cadency to tell sons apart: a label (a bar with hanging points) for the eldest son, a crescent for the second, a mullet (a star) for the third, a martlet (a little bird) for the fourth and an annulet (a ring) for the fifth. A bastard line might bear arms with a visible difference too. A sharp-eyed character who spots a crescent on a stranger's shield and says "a second son" is a lovely bit of world texture.

Show marriages and inheritance on the shield

Real families recorded alliances in their arms. A married couple's arms could be impaled, set side by side on one shield split down the middle, with the husband's on the dexter side (the viewer's left). When a woman with no brothers inherited, her children could quarter her family's arms with their father's, so a quartered shield can hint at a merged inheritance and, sometimes, a disputed claim. The wedding crest guide shows the per pale division in practice.

Badges: what the servants and soldiers wear

A full coat of arms belonged to the lord and the family. Their followers often wore a simpler badge instead, a single emblem worn on clothing or banners. King Richard II of England used a white hart, and in the Wilton Diptych, a painted altarpiece from his reign, even the angels around the Virgin wear white hart badges. For fiction this is gold: a badge makes it easy to put a whole household, garrison or gang in the same scene and have readers tell them apart. Give each major house both arms and a badge, and use the badge for the people who serve them.

Describing arms in prose

Heralds describe arms in a compact language called the blazon, such as "Sable, a chevron Or, overall a raven Or". It is precise but slows a scene down. A good habit is to use the formal blazon once, perhaps in a herald's mouth, an appendix or a map key, and plain words everywhere else: "the gold raven on black". The how to read a blazon guide explains the order and the words if you want your heralds to sound right.

Name the colors consistently, too. If House Varrow is purple and silver on page ten, it should be purple and silver on page four hundred, on the map and on the cover.

Your world does not have to be European

Shields with tinctures and beasts are one tradition among several. Japan developed its own family emblems, called mon: compact designs, often in a circle, worn on clothing and shown on banners and belongings. If your setting draws on a different culture, it may be more convincing to invent an emblem system that fits that culture than to give everyone a European shield. The same storytelling jobs still apply: identity, inheritance and loyalty you can see.

Questions

How do I design a coat of arms for a fantasy novel?

Start with how arms work in your world: who may bear them, who registers them and whether they are inherited. Then design each shield with a strong metal-on-color contrast, one main emblem that suits the house (a pun on the name works well), a motto and a simpler badge for its followers.

What rules should fantasy heraldry follow?

The most useful real rule is the rule of tincture: put a metal (gold or silver) on a color, or a color on a metal, so the shield reads from a distance. Real heraldry also marked younger sons with small cadency marks and showed marriages by splitting the shield down the middle, which both translate well to fiction.

Can I use arms I design in Crest Studio in my published book?

Yes. Once purchased, designs can be used for any lawful purpose, personal or commercial, including maps, chapter headers, websites and merchandise. The designs are not exclusive, so if one will be central to your cover, discuss it with your designer or publisher.

How should I describe a coat of arms in my writing?

Use the formal blazon sparingly, for a herald, an appendix or a map key, and plain description in scenes: "the gold raven on black". Keep each house's colors and emblem consistent across text, maps and cover.

Do all fantasy cultures need European-style shields?

No. Heraldry is one tradition among several. Japan, for example, developed family emblems called mon. A setting based on another culture may feel more convincing with an emblem system of its own.
