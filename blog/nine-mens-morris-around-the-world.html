Nine Men's Morris Around the World: Mills, Merels and Morabaraba

The same board of three squares turns up under many names, carved into stone and cut into turf. Here is what those names mean, how the close relatives differ, and which of the famous stories hold up.

Mills and Mühle

In English the game is also called mills or the mill game, after the three-in-a-row that takes a piece. German speakers call it Mühle, which means mill, and the same idea runs through many other European names. The row of three is the heart of the game, so it is no surprise that so many languages named the whole game after it.

Merels, merrills and morris

Older English names include merels, merrills, merelles and morelles. The game writer Daniel King traces them, and “morris” with them, to the Latin merellus, a counter or gaming piece. A link to morris dancing has been suggested, but it is speculation, and the gaming-piece origin is the better supported one. Nine men's morris, then, means something close to “nine pieces”, which is exactly what each player has.

Navakankari

In India the game is known as Navakankari, from the Sanskrit nava, nine, and kankari, pebbles: nine pebbles for each player. The Gujarati form is Navkaankari, and other Indian languages have names of their own. Boards are drawn on the ground and played with stones or shells, which is how the game has traveled everywhere: all it needs is lines and two colors of pebble.

Morabaraba: twelve cows

Morabaraba is a close relative played in Southern Africa, especially in South Africa and Botswana, with a variant in Lesotho. It is almost the same as twelve men's morris: each player has twelve pieces, called cows, and the board adds diagonal lines between the corners of the squares. The name comes from the Southern Sotho moraba-raba, to go round in a circle. Morabaraba is popular with young people in the countryside, it has an organized competitive scene, and Mind Sports South Africa, its national body, is recognized by the South African government's sports department.

Twelve men's morris

The larger cousin gives each player twelve pieces and adds the four diagonal lines. Twenty-four pieces on twenty-four points means the board can fill completely during placing, and in some rule sets a full board is a draw. Nine men's morris has no diagonals, and nine pieces each leaves six points free, so there is always room to move.

What the history really shows

  • Egypt, maybe. Boards cut into roofing slabs at the temple of Kurna have been dated to about 1400 BCE, but some of the same carvings include Coptic crosses, and the historian Friedrich Berger concluded they cannot be dated. Treat any “3,000 years old” claim with care.
  • Rome. Boards cut into Roman buildings suggest the game was well known there, and Ovid may mention it around 8 CE.
  • The Vikings. A game board from the Gokstad ship burial in Norway has Nine Men's Morris on one side and a tafl game on the other.
  • Medieval England. Boards are carved into cloister seats in several English cathedrals, and large boards were cut into village greens. Shakespeare's Titania complains in A Midsummer Night's Dream that the nine men's morris is filled up with mud.

The rules most people play today, including flying when a player is down to three pieces, are the common modern ones. Older and regional rule sets differ in the details, which is part of the fun of meeting the game somewhere new.
